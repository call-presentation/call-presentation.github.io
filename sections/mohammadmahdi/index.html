Introduction

Imagine being told that a human being is described as "His judge among His creation."

At first, that sounds powerful — maybe even controversial. Does that mean a human shares in God's authority? Or does it mean something deeper?

In Shia Islam, especially within Twelver thought, the Imams are believed to be divinely appointed successors to Prophet Muhammad. They are not prophets, and they are not divine. But they are understood to be chosen by God to guide, interpret, and uphold justice after the Prophet's death.

The Hadith: "His Judge Among His Creation"

So when a Shia hadith describes the Imam as "His judge among His creation," it doesn't mean the Imam replaces God. It means the Imam carries out judgment on God's behalf — applying divine law with knowledge and justice granted by Allah.

The authority is delegated, not independent.

The Qur'anic Principle

Now let's look at a Qur'anic verse from Surah Hud, verse 86, where Prophet Shuʿayb tells his people:

"What remains of Allah's provision is better for you, should you be faithful, and I am not a keeper over you."

Here, the prophet clearly draws a line. He delivers the message, but he does not control people. He is not their ultimate authority. That belongs to Allah alone.

Reconciling the Two

So how do these two ideas fit together?

The key is understanding roles. In Shia theology, God's sovereignty is absolute. But throughout history, God works through chosen representatives — prophets, and after them, Imams. Just as Prophet Shuʿayb guided without claiming divine status, the Imam judges without claiming divine independence.

The Imam is seen as the channel of divine justice — not its source.

So rather than competing with the Qur'anic principle that God alone has ultimate authority, this hadith reflects a structured hierarchy:

  • God as the supreme judge
  • The Imam as His appointed executor of justice among people

Conclusion

And that distinction — between ultimate authority and delegated authority — is essential to understanding Shia theology.

The Imam does not replace God's sovereignty. Rather, the Imam serves as the appointed vessel through which divine justice is enacted in the world — a role that complements, rather than contradicts, the Qur'anic insistence on God's absolute authority.
