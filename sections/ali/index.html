Overview

The presentation does some things well: it quotes the Bible directly, admits what it doesn't cover, and explains the Jewish historical background. Many presentations skip these basics.

But there are three problems that Bible scholars would notice. You don't need religious beliefs to understand these issues.

1) Ignoring Bible verses that contradict the main point

Matthew 24, Mark 13, and Luke 21 say two opposite things:

  • Some verses describe far-off cosmic events (Matt 24:30; Mark 13:26–27)
  • Other verses say it will happen soon: "this generation will not pass away" (Matt 24:34; Mark 13:30; Luke 21:32)

The presentation only quotes the first type and says it's "a future event" with "unknown timing." But that ignores the verses right next to them that say it will happen soon.

Better way to say it:

"The New Testament describes a 'coming' event using dramatic language about the sky and visible to everyone on earth (Matt 24:30; Luke 21:27; Acts 1:11). But these same chapters also say it will happen within 'this generation' (Matt 24:34). So the timing is both 'unknown' (Matt 24:36) and 'soon.'"

2) "Son of Man coming on clouds" — misunderstanding the source

This phrase comes from Daniel 7:13–14. In that old text, a figure "like a son of man" rides clouds and goes up to God's throne to get power — going up, not coming down to earth.

Many Bible experts think when Jesus said "Son of Man coming on clouds," he meant he would be raised to a high position by God — not that he would physically fly down from the sky. Experts still argue about whether this already happened in the first century or will happen in the future.

Better way to say it:

"The 'Son of Man' idea comes from Daniel 7:13–14. In the New Testament, this figure comes with clouds, appears before God, gets power, sends angels, and judges people (Matt 24:30–31; Mark 13:26–27). In the original Daniel story, the figure goes up to God's throne, not down to earth. Experts argue about whether the New Testament means a first-century event or something in the future."

3) Making it sound simpler than it actually is

The presentation talks about "one clear plan" for "one future event." But the New Testament has many different ideas:

  • Revelation describes several different events happening one after another (Rev 19:11–16; 20:11–15).
  • Paul's letters connect Jesus's return with people rising from the dead (1 Cor 15:23, 51–54; 1 Thess 4:16–17).
  • Judgment scenes (Matt 25:31–46; Rev 20:11–15) describe different details. Treating them as the same thing misses important differences.

Better way to say it:

"These Bible passages have some common ideas: dramatic sky events, a powerful central figure, judgment based on what people did, unclear timing, and warnings about fake messiahs. They use symbolic picture-language that experts argue about — does it mean historical events or future events? This presentation doesn't cover everything about the end times (like Paul's teachings on resurrection or Revelation chapters 19–22)."

Small fixes

  • Instead of "public, visible, and global," say: "described using big dramatic language about everyone seeing it." Bible writers often used sky/cosmic language even when talking about regular historical events (see Isaiah 13:10; 34:4; Joel 2:10, 31).
  • Instead of "ethical conduct," just say "what people did" or "people's actions" (that's what the Greek word actually means in Matt 25:31–46 and Rev 20:12–13). "Ethical conduct" sounds like modern philosophy.

Bottom Line

With these fixes, the presentation sticks to facts, avoids taking religious sides, covers more material, and meets basic standards — making it work for different audiences.
